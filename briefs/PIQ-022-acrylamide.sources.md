| Claim | Source | URL | Date checked |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acrylamide is found mainly in foods made from plants, such as potato products, grain products, or coffee; FDA notes boiling and steaming do not typically produce it (frying, baking and roasting do) | FDA, "Acrylamide Questions and Answers" | https://www.fda.gov/food/process-contaminants-food/acrylamide-questions-and-answers | 2026-09-28 |

Notes
- Frame as divergence/nuance, not "stricter is better": California itself carved out an exception for coffee specifically while leaving the general Prop 65 warning requirement in place for other acrylamide-containing foods; FDA has no limit for any of them, just voluntary industry guidance.
- Did not use a specific NSRL (No Significant Risk Level) micrograms/day figure for acrylamide -- OEHHA's own history here is genuinely ambiguous across sources found (a 1990 codified value of 0.2 microgram/day, later 2005 and 2019/2020 proposals of 1.0 and 1.1 micrograms/day; which is the current codified regulatory NSRL as of today was not confirmed with confidence from a direct primary fetch -- oehha.ca.gov pages returned empty content on WebFetch every time they were tried). Not used as a claim. The brief instead uses the coffee-specific regulation (27 CCR 25704), which was confirmed directly.
- Do not name specific brands. This is about the acrylamide-formation-and-labeling gap generally, not about which chip or coffee brand has how much.
- "Guidance for Industry: Acrylamide in Foods" is FDA's actual document title, referenced but not quoted verbatim beyond the Q&A page's own description of it (the guidance document itself was not fetched directly).
